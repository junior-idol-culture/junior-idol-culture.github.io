About this project

Junior Idol Culture is a reference site on junior idols and Japan’s culture of young performers. Rather than adopting a single polemical position, it considers performers’ wishes and dignity, cultural context, and the specific nature of individual activities.

A junior idol is a term used within Japanese idol culture for an idol who begins or conducts their activities at a young age. Its usage and age range have varied according to period, medium, and field of entertainment. During the 1990s, the expression “Jr. idol” was used broadly enough to include elementary- and middle-school-aged child actors and young talents.[1] For conceptual clarity, this article uses junior idol as a general term for a person under the age of eighteen who works as an idol or engages in idol-like entertainment activities.

The activities of junior idols may extend beyond singing, dancing, and live performance to acting, modelling, voice acting, television and advertising appearances, photography, and online communication. The category partly overlaps with child actors, teenage models, young singers, and gravure idols, but it is not synonymous with any one of them. Being a junior idol does not necessarily imply participation in gravure.

Japanese idol culture may place value not only on completed skills and works but also on development through activity, the formation of an individual personality, accumulated effort, and a continuing relationship with fans. Junior idols can be understood as performers who participate in public forms of expression during childhood or adolescence and share their experiences and development with an audience.

Because the performers are still developing, careful consideration must be given to age-appropriate working conditions, the balance between activities, education, and private life, privacy, the performer’s wishes, and the long-term handling of images and records. Discussion of junior idols should neither reject young people’s participation in entertainment as a whole nor automatically characterize it as sexual. The particular activity, production environment, expressive context, and wishes and dignity of the performer should be considered individually.

Fields of Activity and Management of Junior Idols

Junior idols in Japan work through a variety of organizations, including entertainment agencies, modelling agencies, theatre companies, training institutions, music-production companies, and organizations managing local idol groups. Some Japanese talent agencies have been discovering and training newcomers and managing performers for several decades.[2]

Entertainment Activities of Junior Idols

Activities commonly associated with junior idols include singing, dancing, live appearances, and participation in idol groups or musical units. In practice, their activities may extend further, including television dramas, films, stage productions, commercials, fashion and advertising modelling, voice acting, narration, radio, events, and photographic works.

A junior idol may perform songs and dances as a member of a group while also appearing in television dramas or commercials and working as a magazine model or voice actor. This crossing of professional fields is related to the fact that, in Japan, an “idol” is not merely a musical classification. It can also be a cultural position involving a performer’s personality, public communication, development, and relationship with an audience.

Modelling can take several forms, including fashion, advertising, magazines, product promotion, portraiture, and gravure. Its content and purpose differ according to the medium, project, costume, method of photography, and intended audience.

Forms of Affiliation among Junior Idols

Many junior idols belong to entertainment agencies or training institutions and receive assistance with lessons, auditions, publicity, appearance negotiations, and schedule management. Some begin through local idol groups, theatre companies, dance schools, or small entertainment agencies. Others pursue individual or family-managed activities with the assistance of a parent or guardian.

Junior Idols and Online Activity

Online communication is one of the principal means through which junior idols present their activities and maintain relationships with audiences and fans. Social-media and video services such as X, Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube may be used to publish photographs, short videos, singing and dance performances, appearance information, and everyday messages.

Live-streaming services such as SHOWROOM, MixChannel, and Niconico Live also intersect with the activities of idols, models, singers, and voice actors.[3] Accounts may be managed by performers, agencies, group management, or parents and guardians. For younger performers, assistance with visibility settings, comments, personal information, and the preservation of records may form part of responsible management.

The Prehistory of Junior Idols and Japan’s Culture of Young Performers

The term “junior idol,” and the modern forms of activity it denotes, arose against the background of the modern entertainment industry and mass media. It would be inappropriate to apply the term directly to child performers of the early modern period or earlier. Nevertheless, the participation of children and adolescents in singing, dancing, theatre, and other forms of public expression in Japan has a history older than the contemporary idol industry.

Traditional Performing Arts and Young Performers

Japanese traditional performing arts contain examples of children appearing on stage and learning acting, singing, and dancing. Kabuki includes roles performed by actual children, who may acquire experience through training in speech, movement, and Japanese dance.[4]

Child performers in traditional arts and contemporary junior idols are not identical in their institutions, purposes, or audiences. They nevertheless possess comparable cultural elements insofar as young performers receive training, appear before the public, and broaden their expressive abilities as they grow.

The appeal of a young performer is not based solely on physical appearance. Costume, voice, gesture, facial expression, suitability for a role, stage presence, freshness, future potential, and visible change through experience may combine to form a complete performance. These values differ among periods and fields and should not be treated as a single, unchanging Japanese “aesthetic of youth.”

Modern Girls’ Revue and Cinema

The Takarazuka Girls’ Revue presented its first performance on April 1, 1914, with seventeen girls between the ages of twelve and seventeen.[5] It developed a theatrical culture combining singing, dancing, acting, costume, and star appeal, together with the systematic training of young performers.

Takarazuka cannot simply be declared the direct origin of the contemporary junior idol. It is nevertheless a useful precedent because young performers learned several fields of performance and individual performers became objects of continuing attention and support. Academic research has also examined contemporary magazine representations of Takarazuka performers as “idols.”[6]

Film allowed the same performer’s image and acting to be shown repeatedly in different locations, enabling audiences to follow a performer’s development through multiple works. Hideko Takamine first appeared in the film Mother in 1929 at the age of five and continued acting into adulthood.[7]

Continuity and Change Leading to Contemporary Junior-Idol Culture

There is no single, uninterrupted lineage connecting prewar child actors, girl singers, revue performers, and film stars with junior idols of the postwar period. Each arose under different institutions, industries, educational ideas, and media technologies. Certain elements have nevertheless appeared repeatedly: young people participating in several forms of expression, performers developing through training and experience, and audiences maintaining an interest in their personalities and development.

The Formation of Junior-Idol Culture in Postwar Japan

The environment leading to contemporary junior-idol culture developed alongside the postwar growth of cinema, radio, recorded music, television, magazines, and entertainment agencies. Young performers increasingly worked across several media, and their personalities and development became part of how they were publicly known.

Hibari Misora and the Postwar Girl Star

Hibari Misora was a representative girl star who appeared before the term “idol” became established in its modern Japanese sense. She debuted at the age of nine in 1946. In 1949, she appeared in film and made her first records, becoming widely known through the success of the film and song Kanashiki Kuchibue.[8]

Calling Hibari Misora a junior idol without qualification would be historically anachronistic. Nevertheless, her pattern of beginning at a young age, combining singing with acting, and allowing audiences to follow her development over many years provides an important precedent for understanding later junior-idol culture.

The Television Era and Idols Who Were Minors

As television became common in Japanese households, singers appeared continually through music programs, dramas, films, commercials, and magazines as well as through records. From the 1970s onward, young idols supported not only for singing and acting but also for their approachable personalities, fashion, personal narratives, and development became major figures in Japanese entertainment culture.

Momoe Yamaguchi made her singing debut with Toshigoro in May 1973 at the age of fourteen and subsequently worked as an actress alongside her musical activities.[9] Akina Nakamori debuted with Slow Motion on May 1, 1982, at the age of sixteen.[10]

These examples demonstrate that many prominent idols began working while still minors and that this period formed an important part of their careers. Being a minor was not merely a peripheral exception within idol culture but a recurring feature of its history.

Junior-Idol Groups and a Culture of Training

From the 1990s onward, group-based idol activities founded on auditions, trainee systems, dance lessons, and regular performances became increasingly prominent. Such groups sometimes included elementary- and middle-school students.

The four tenth-generation members announced by Morning Musume in 2011 were eleven, twelve, fourteen, and sixteen years old, an average of 13.25. The official figure of 15.8 referred to the average age of the full twelve-member group following Ai Takahashi’s graduation.[11]

In this kind of system, selection, admission, practice, first appearances, and changing roles become visible parts of a performer’s history. A culture of supporting development may find value not only in a future completed performer but also in present effort, experimentation, personality, and forms of expression that exist only at a particular stage.

Junior Idols and a Culture of Development and Support

Within junior-idol culture, not only completed singing, acting, and dance performances but also the process through which performers develop their skills and individuality may be understood as part of their expression.

The Development of Junior Idols

Japanese idol culture has been discussed as placing value not only on technical completion but also on the approachability and visible change associated with performers who are still developing.[12] Research on idol-audition programs has likewise examined the process through which ordinary young people are selected, trained, and represented as stars.[13]

The value of junior idols should not be understood solely as an intermediate stage leading toward a completed adult performer. Their voices, expressions, sensibilities, efforts, and relationships at a particular time possess forms that cannot later be reproduced in precisely the same way.

Activities Supporting Junior Idols

Support may include attending performances, listening to music, watching videos, purchasing works, sending messages, participating in voting projects, and introducing activities through social media. Continuing support may also produce interaction and a sense of community among fans. Research has reported relationships between identification with an idol, fellowship with other fans, continued support, and fans’ well-being.[14]

Support does not imply ownership of a performer or a right to determine their career. Decisions concerning activities and the future should centre on the performer’s own wishes, with age-appropriate assistance from parents, guardians, and affiliated organizations.

Shared Time and Records

Regular performances, seasonal events, releases, anniversaries, school progression, and changing roles give an activity continuity over time. Earlier photographs, videos, songs, costumes, and blog entries record changes in the performer and may also become connected with the audience’s memories of the period.

Junior-idol status is necessarily limited by age. This limited duration gives the activity a distinctive rhythm: performers and audiences know that growth and transition will occur, while the expressions of each period are recorded, supported, and remembered.

Junior Idols, Gravure, and Photographic Expression

Within Japanese idol culture, photography is not merely a means of communicating appearance information. It is also one of the principal media through which an idol’s individuality and atmosphere are expressed.

The Scope of Junior-Idol Gravure

The term “gravure” originally referred to an intaglio printing process and subsequently became established in Japanese as a term for photographic pages in magazines. In entertainment, modelling centred on magazine photo pages, photo books, and posters is commonly described as gravure.[15]

The word does not in itself denote only one type of clothing or form of expression. Person-centred photography can include portraiture, fashion, advertising, product promotion, stage-costume photography, and works based on travel or seasonal themes.

Not every junior idol participates in gravure, nor is every photograph of a junior idol gravure. Publicity photographs, fashion pages, documentary images of performances, and everyday social-media posts have different purposes and contexts. When junior idols model for magazines, photo books, or digital photographic works, the content may range from everyday fashion to swimwear used at beaches or pools, depending on age, medium, editorial policy, and intended readership.

The Photographic Expression of Junior Idols

Costume, light, colour, background, season, location, facial expression, and posture combine to create a particular impression. Photography records a real person, but it is also an expression constructed through location, clothing, lighting, camera distance, direction, editing, and selection.

Postwar idol magazines communicated performers’ personalities and everyday public images by publishing photographs alongside information about music and screen appearances. Myojo, for example, developed from a postwar popular-entertainment magazine into a publication closely associated with idol culture from the 1970s onward.[16]

Photo Books as Records of Junior Idols

A photo book collects images of one performer or group according to a particular theme and editorial approach. A junior idol’s photo book is produced as a work but can also preserve a particular age or period of activity. Once published, however, photographs can be copied, preserved, and recirculated, requiring careful management of privacy, wishes, and long-term control over images.

Junior Idols and Image Videos

An image video is intended to communicate the impression or atmosphere of a person or product emotionally through moving images rather than through direct description.[17] Works featuring idols may combine indoor and outdoor footage with conversation, interviews, hobbies, singing, dancing, fashion, travel, or production scenes.

Some image videos centre on gravure, but the form is not inherently limited to gravure. Photographs and videos may simultaneously function as publicity, performance, modelling, creative works, and records of a career. Their content should be understood from the actual expression, method of production, form of distribution, and the performer’s age and wishes—not solely from labels.

Protection, Expression, and Social Debate Concerning Junior Idols

Junior idols are minors who require age-appropriate protection, but they are also performers with aspirations and objectives of their own. Protection and agency are not opposing principles. Appropriate protection provides the conditions under which performers can choose their activities and express themselves with confidence.

The Wishes and Agency of Junior Idols

Discussion often centres on surrounding adults: parents, agencies, production staff, fans, and critics. The words of performers themselves must also be respected according to age and development. Japan’s Basic Act on Children and the Convention on the Rights of the Child affirm children’s opportunities to express their views and the consideration due to those views according to age and maturity.[18]

A junior idol’s wish to sing, dance, act, or participate in photography as a model is an important fact. Such wishes should not be presumed false, manipulated, or uninformed solely because the performer is a minor. Parental permission alone, however, does not establish the performer’s own willingness. Performers should receive age-appropriate explanations and be able to ask questions, refuse, or change their minds.

When performers describe their activities positively, outsiders may undermine their agency by insisting that they “must really have been victims.” Conversely, when performers later wish to distance themselves from earlier activities, their present wishes should also be respected.

The Roles of Parents, Agencies, and Production Staff

The role of adults and organizations is not to make every decision in place of the performer, but to establish an environment in which the performer can understand and choose. This can include coordinating education and rest, safe transportation, health, payment, contracts, accompaniment, and personal information.

When entertainment activities legally constitute employment, provisions protecting young workers also apply. Employment of children who have not completed compulsory education is generally restricted, exceptional work in film and theatre is subject to conditions, and a parent or guardian may not conclude an employment contract on behalf of a minor.[19]

Debate Concerning Photographs and Videos of Junior Idols

Debate includes the age-appropriateness of clothing and presentation, direction that creates a sexual impression, intended audiences, recirculation, unauthorized copying or alteration, and digital records that remain after adulthood. An identifiable photograph may constitute personal information, and Japan’s Personal Information Protection Commission has discussed consent as a desirable measure when photographs are made available to an unspecified public.[20]

The Act on Regulation and Punishment of Acts Relating to Child Prostitution and Child Pornography is not intended to regulate minors’ bodies or expression in general. Its stated purpose is to protect children’s rights from sexual exploitation and sexual abuse. In applying the Act, care must also be taken not to unduly infringe rights and freedoms relating to academic research, cultural and artistic activity, journalism, and similar fields, or to depart from that protective purpose.[21]

Under the Act, classification as “child pornography” is not determined solely by the subject’s being under eighteen or by clothing such as swimwear. Whether a work falls within one of the categories specifically defined by law must be assessed in light of its overall content, composition, direction, and manner of presentation. Ordinary entertainment photographs, portraits, fashion photographs, and works involving swimwear therefore do not automatically fall within the statute merely because of the performer’s age, clothing, or the label attached to the work.

Criticism that ignores the work, production process, and performer’s wishes and instead centres entirely on a sexual interpretation imposed by the viewer may itself reduce the performer to a one-dimensional sexualized figure. The possibility that a junior idol participates as entertainment, fashion, photography, acting, or personal expression should not be excluded in advance.

Perspectives Supporting Junior-Idol Activities

Supporters may identify value in experience in music, acting, dance, or modelling; the achievement of completing a work; personal expression; confidence and skills; preparation for a later career; and the preservation of a record of a particular period. Photographs and videos may be appreciated as cultural or artistic works expressing individuality, fashion, season, atmosphere, and development.

Such support does not require unconditional approval of every project. Supporting activities genuinely desired by the performer is compatible with requiring an environment in which inappropriate requests can be refused, reliable people are available for consultation, contracts are transparent, and production policies are appropriate to the performer’s age.

Individual Evaluation of Junior-Idol Activities

Junior-idol activities cannot readily be evaluated uniformly according to age or the name of the activity alone. Relevant questions include what the performer wants, whether they understand the activity, whether they can refuse or request changes, whether education and health are protected, whether publication is appropriate, and how images and records will be managed.

It is inappropriate either to disregard a performer’s wishes in the name of protection or to remove the responsibilities of surrounding adults in the name of those wishes. Discussion should begin by respecting each junior idol as an individual performer with agency and by considering the environment supporting their activities.

Graduation from Junior-Idol Status and Later Life

Under the definition used in this article, a performer ceases to fall within the age category of junior idol upon reaching eighteen. This does not necessarily mean that their entertainment career or status as an idol ends.

From Junior Idol to Adult Performer

Experience gained in singing, acting, dancing, photography, stage performance, and online broadcasting may continue to influence an adult career. Eighteen is an important legal and social boundary, but a performer’s development is not completed on that date. The transition is often a gradual change in roles, expression, responsibility, and relationships with audiences.

Graduation from a Group or Activity

Within idol culture, “graduation” may also describe departure from a group, training system, program, project, or agency. It may occur because of education, employment, health, family circumstances, or a different creative direction. Some activities conclude with a graduation performance; others end gradually without a formal farewell.

Choosing to Leave Entertainment

A person who worked as a junior idol does not necessarily remain in entertainment as an adult. Leaving entertainment does not necessarily reject earlier experiences, and earlier public support does not create an obligation to remain a public performer indefinitely.

Earlier Works and Present Wishes

Photographs, videos, music, screen appearances, blogs, and social-media posts may remain available after activities have ended. Former junior idols differ in how they understand earlier work. Evaluation should respect both the performer’s wishes at the time and their wishes in the present. When an adult speaks positively about earlier work, outsiders should not dismiss that evaluation automatically; when a performer seeks distance, that choice should likewise be respected.

Junior-Idol Status as a Limited Period

The period during which a person is a junior idol necessarily ends with age. Its temporary nature does not make the activity less meaningful. The voices, expressions, abilities, friendships, performances, and works of a limited period cannot later be reproduced in exactly the same form.

Graduation from junior-idol status is therefore not merely an ending but a transition. Whether performers continue in entertainment or follow a different path, the time spent as junior idols remains part of their later lives and of the cultural record.
